Social Returns to Private Choice? Effects of Charter Schools on Behavioral Outcomes, Arrests, and Civic Participation

Andrew McEachin; Douglas Lee Lauen; Sarah Crittenden Fuller; Rachel M. Perera

School choice & charterAttendanceSafety & well-being

What the study found

Students entering charter schools from traditional public schools had favorable absence, suspension, conviction, and voting estimates. Results were less consistent for students already in the charter sector.

Considerations for practice

Distinguish entry decisions and outcomes beyond test scores when examining school choice.

Limitations and interpretation

Nonrandom sector choices may leave unmeasured differences. Historical cohorts and linked NC records limit generalization to current schools and people outside the records.

Questions that remain

Do patterns persist for recent entrants and different school models?

Study at a glance

Design
Observational comparison with inverse-probability weighting
NC setting & population
NC ninth-grade cohorts entering high school in 2004–05 through 2011–12; linked adult records.

Agenda questions this work informs

Priority 1EmergingRA-006

School-sector results differ across outcomes and entry decisions.

School-sector pathways

Charter research adds behavioral and civic outcomes for historical ninth-grade entrants. The early Opportunity Scholarship study reports positive estimates with strong volunteer-sample and test-comparability limits. Neither establishes current sector-wide superiority.

Priority 4SubstantialRA-041

Attendance matters, and its causes and remedies differ.

Attendance

NC studies document persistent absence, learning associations, and particular healthcare or policy pathways. A historical charter-entry study adds favorable attendance estimates for that choice context; it does not establish a universal attendance intervention.

Priority 4EmergingRA-042

Discipline decisions can change without worsening average outcomes.

Safety and discipline

Early-grade reform and disparity studies address exclusion and fairness. A separate historical charter-entry study adds behavioral outcomes, but does not identify which discipline practices explain its estimates.

Student access and opportunity
