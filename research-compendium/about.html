About the Evidence

A growing guide to NC education findings and the people behind them, designed to help practitioners and policymakers ask informed questions and connect with research partners.

Scope and initial search

The initial search covers work released from October 26, 2016 through October 26, 2026 that uses NC data or directly examines NC education. It includes connected early learning and postsecondary work, journal articles, working papers, evaluations, agency reporting, technical policy studies, and preliminary project findings.

This is a structured starting collection, not a systematic review or a complete inventory. Searches began with NCLRN and the NC Collaboratory, then extended to author and institutional publication pages, SERVE, the Friday Institute, Duke, IES, CALDER, EdWorkingPapers, ERIC, journal publishers, and public agency sources. Search terms combined North Carolina with the existing agenda topics and program names.

The site retains the agenda’s six priorities and 17 topics. Study-to-question mappings are editorial judgments about relevance. Strategic-plan expansion is reserved for later curation rather than adding unapproved priority categories now.

For undated project pages, the directory records the known project cohort and leaves the publication year unknown. A past project end date does not prove that final results have been released. Project-only records do not count as findings.

What the labels mean

Coverage and consistency are separate judgments about the records currently indexed for a question. They are draft editorial classifications, not formal study-quality scores, ESSA tiers, or an agency determination that a question is answered.

Evidence coverage

Substantial
Several distinct NC analyses from more than one research team, including published empirical findings, address meaningful parts of the question. This describes breadth in the collection; causal certainty and completeness may remain limited.
Emerging
Relevant findings are available, but the scope, replication, methods available for review, or maturity of the work limits a broader conclusion. A published study can still contribute to an emerging evidence base.
Project only
A relevant project has been located, but no outcome findings have been reviewed. Its design and contact information are useful; it supplies no effectiveness conclusion.
Not yet indexed
No study is mapped here yet. Further searching and review are needed; absence from this directory is not evidence that research does not exist.

How findings compare

Aligned within indexed evidence
Reviewed findings point in a broadly similar direction for the outcomes discussed. This does not establish unanimity in the literature or a common effect size.
Mixed / context-dependent
Patterns differ across outcomes, populations, program models, or settings. This can reflect meaningful variation rather than contradictory studies.
Limited comparison
Too few directly comparable findings have been reviewed to assess agreement.
Not assessed
The question spans different constructs or the available records do not support a useful consistency judgment.

A record’s publication or project status is separate from these labels. “Working paper” names the version reviewed; it does not certify that no later journal version exists. “Preliminary findings” means that a source explicitly reports early results, often without a complete accessible methods report.

Using findings in a decision

  1. Match the decision to the question. A related study rarely answers every part of a broad priority.
  2. Check the people, place, and period. A recent publication may analyze older data or one district.
  3. Read the design and limitations. Descriptions, associations, and effect estimates answer different questions. A null estimate is not proof that an intervention can never work.
  4. Separate findings from application. Practice considerations and proposed next questions are editorial interpretations, not tested prescriptions.
  5. Connect with the researchers. Ask about current evidence, local fit, implementation, costs, and feasibility before adopting or scaling an approach.

Preliminary numbers are identified as preliminary. Multi-state results are not presented as NC-only findings. Different programs, outcomes, and designs are not ranked as if they were interchangeable.

Sources, provenance, and versions

Each record identifies the source reviewed, the review basis (such as full text, abstract, project summary, or agency summary), the section supporting the findings, the source-check date, and relevant version notes. Full reports were not available or fully reviewed for every entry; that limitation is visible on the record.

Related working papers, briefs, and journal versions are treated as one study family. Annual program evaluations are not automatically counted as independent replications. The current source data include stable study, researcher, and agenda IDs so the collection can grow without breaking existing links.

The original agenda remains the authority for priority names and question wording. Researcher profiles use public professional information and source-reported affiliations. Inclusion does not imply NCLRN membership, endorsement, willingness to collaborate, or current availability.

Stewardship by NCLRN

This draft is prepared for NCLRN to curate and maintain. Suggested additions should include a citation or public source, the NC setting or data used, relevant agenda questions, a findings summary, limitations, and the most current version available.

Before a record moves beyond draft status, an NCLRN reviewer should verify its citation, NC relevance, methods, summary, and mappings, and confirm public researcher contact information. Revise question syntheses when new findings change the balance of evidence, recording the date and reviewer. Recheck undated preliminary summaries for final reports.
